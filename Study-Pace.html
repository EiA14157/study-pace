THE STUDY NOTES

A better plan starts with better assumptions.

01 / ESTIMATE

How many pages can you really study in an hour?

A page count is a workload estimate. A dense textbook chapter and a familiar review handout are different tasks.

Read the note

Time a short, representative sample. Read five pages the way you intend to study: pause for worked examples, jot down questions, and check what you can explain without looking. Divide the elapsed minutes by five and use that as your starting estimate.

If five pages take 20 minutes, enter 4 minutes per page. With 240 pages remaining, that is 960 minutes of new material. It does not include every break, a full practice exam, or a long final review.

For recorded classes, use lectures only when their lengths and difficulty are reasonably similar. Otherwise, make equal-sized chunks and use practice sets as your unit. After two sessions, update your estimate with what actually happened.

02 / REMEMBER

Give review its own place on the calendar.

Finishing the material and remembering it are different goals. Plan for both before the last night arrives.

Read the note

Start each study session with a few questions from an earlier topic, answered from memory before opening your notes. Check your answers and return to what you missed. This turns review into something you do throughout the plan.

The final review days in this calculator are extra protected time. Use them for mixed practice, explaining ideas aloud, or revisiting an error log. They are not a substitute for revisiting older material along the way.

Spacing study and practicing retrieval are supported by learning research. The best spacing depends on the material and how long you need to retain it; this tool does not prescribe a scientifically optimal interval.

Reading: Institute of Education Sciences: Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning.

03 / ADJUST

Missed a day? Recalculate before you cram.

A useful plan has somewhere for a missed session to go. Catch-up days make that trade-off visible.

Read the note

First, count only the work that remains. Set a new start date, keep the exam date, and rebuild the plan. Do not add yesterday’s unfinished work twice.

If the busiest day exceeds your budget, consider a genuine change: another available study day, a more realistic time budget, or a smaller set of material agreed with your instructor. Moving numbers around does not create extra time.

The calculator puts catch-up days after new material and before final review. You can use that reserved time earlier if life gets in the way, then recalculate. If you stay on schedule, use it for weak topics or rest rather than automatically adding more work.

Try keeping at least one margin day when the calendar allows. This is a practical planning suggestion, not a research-backed minimum.

ABOUT STUDY PACE

A countdown with a plan attached.

Inspired by deadline-first study planning, Study Pace is a small, independent tool for students who want a usable plan without setting up another productivity system.

How the calculation works

We count selected weekdays from the start date through the day before the exam. Then we reserve your catch-up and final review days. Remaining units are spread as evenly as possible across new-material days, with larger sessions first. “Minutes on busiest day” equals the largest unit count multiplied by your minutes-per-unit estimate, rounded up.

The time-budget check covers new material only. Review and catch-up days are reserved at your chosen daily budget; their actual workload is not estimated. The plan supports dates up to two years apart and whole units only. If there are fewer units than study days, unused new-material days become light review days.
